The Family People's Business

The 1987 Constitution banned political dynasties; but for 37 years, no one ever thought to disambiguate that term.

The standard

What governance is supposed to be

Having a set of rules is different from whether citizens can actually use them. The frame the course leans on names four pillars, and every one assumes power is answerable and shared.

01
Transparency; information is open and reachable
02
Accountability; officials answer for their decisions
03
Predictability; laws apply consistently to everyone
04
Participation; people shape decisions that affect them

Add checks and balances and decentralization- moving power "away from the center" to widen the door to participation. Hold onto this thought!

The problem

Then a few families walked through the door and locked it

A political dynasty is a family that keeps elected power across its members- either in sequence, or all at once. This happens all over the world, in varying degrees. But in the Philippines, it is not a fringe edge case- it is one of its most normalized systems.

The distinction

Not all dynasties look the same

Ateneo and AIM economist Ronald Mendoza separates thin from fat dynasties- and it is the fat ones that more often correlate with real damage.

Thin dynasty

One seat, passed down over time

A single post across generations. Surprisingly common and not automatically harmful.

Fat dynasty "sabay-sabay"

Many relatives, many seats, simultaneously

Mayor, governor, congressman- all cousins. Tied to deeper systemic poverty.

The Chicken and The egg

Do dynasties cause poverty, or does poverty breed dynasties?

Strong evidence that poverty entrenches dynasties; weaker that dynasties worsen it- though a clear worsening effect shows up in provinces outside Luzon.

So dynasties are less analogous to a disease than a symptom that worsens the sickness of bad governance. They corrode the pillars directly: checks and balances cannot work inside a fat dynasty- you cannot hold your own brother accountable- and dynasties thrive under decentralization, capturing the very local power that was meant to be spread around. Gerrymandering, like the attempt to split Palawan into three, can even multiply seats for the same clans.

A dynasty is not bad because of its last name. A leader is effective or not on their own merit, bloodline be damned.

The true danger of a dynasty is its toxic realization. Its purest form is a country where the ballot is merely a formality- the Kims in North Korea, power as literal inheritance. We may be relieved that this is far from the reality of the Philippines, yet we have our own quieter machinery. This is the reason the sentiment to "just vote them out" rarely ever works.

The reason the cycle holds

Utang na loob, at the ballot box

My own hunch was that people keep backing the same dynasties even when the evidence says those dynasties are failing them- out of ignorance, a sense of belonging, or a vote that got bought. And this hunch has a name in the readings.

Mendoza describes an exchange: in poor areas with few real opportunities, people trade their political capital- their vote, their loyalty- for immediate help. And this can take on the form of a bag of rice for the upcoming week, or a job to secure a semi-decent livelihood for the future. The dynasty then collects on that debt of gratitude come election time. Poverty and dynasty feed on each other and grow, and the votes that are meant to shape our nation are turned into little thank-you notes.

The people most failed by entrenched power are the ones most bound to keep electing it.

The reckoning

A ban with no teeth- until this year

Article II, Section 26 says the State "shall… prohibit political dynasties." The catch is the next six words: as may be defined by law. And the Congress meant to write that law was 70% dynasties.

  • 1987

    The Constitution bans dynasties- but outsources the definition to Congress.

  • 1987–2024

    More than 100 anti-dynasty bills are filed. Every single one of those bills dies. The Supreme Court keeps calling the ban "not self-executing."

  • Aug 26, 2026

    In the Kapatiran case, a unanimous Court reverses course: Section 26 is a mandatory duty. Congress must act "with utmost urgency."

The case study

So what happens when a dynasty actually falls?

You do not need a hypothetical. Look at Pasig- the city I am proud to be from.

For 27 years, from 1992, Pasig was a textbook example of a fat dynasty. The mayoralty passed from Bobby Eusebio to his wife Maribel and back, while his brother Richard continued to sit in Congress. The seats rotated among the family- sabay-sabay, by the book.

In 2019 a 29-year-old first-term councilor named Vico Sotto ran against all of it as an independent, and won. He had studied political science at the Ateneo and taken his master's at the Ateneo School of Government- and he campaigned on one word: participation.

My verdict

Bloodline is not the variable

My position is not that dynasties are evil; grossly oversimplified, a leader is effective or ineffective on their own merit, whatever may be their last name.

Competent dynastic officials exist, and so do incompetent ones.

And these incompetent ones seem uniquely evil- the family that treats a city as an inheritance and runs it into the ground while still winning every election- with the reason it keeps winning being its most unsettling aspect: people will continue backing the dynasties they already back, regardless of the evidence.

Although Pasig eventually chose differently, the uncomfortable truth is that so many places, year after year, may never.

My critique

Pasig still has to prove itself

I will not pretend the seal makes Pasig perfect. I continue to worry about my city in two ways: first, the reform is built on one person- Sotto is on his final term, and governance that lives or dies with a single reformer has not really been institutionalized yet. Second, the SGLG is a floor, not a ceiling: with 714 winners in one year, it means "passed the standard," not "nothing left to fix." And it could very well be that the floor, the standard is just set too low.

The potholes, the floods, the daily friction are all still there to argue about. Good governance is not a badge you win every three years or so, it is the day-to-day work of staying answerable.

Democracy and governance are at their strongest when they make room for pluralism and participation- when citizens stop waiting to be governed and start insisting on being heard.

— and that is the whole point of civil society.
